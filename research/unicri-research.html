The straight answer

You were right. I did the ACAPS review properly and gave UNICRI two paragraphs. Having now read the challenge repo file by file and the nineteen papers behind it, that was a sequencing mistake — this track has roughly ten times the published evidence base of the ACAPS one, and the repo contains material I had not opened, including a research folder with the organisers' own evidence summaries and reading list.

Three things change my read of the two challenges.

1

This track is not evidence-poor. It is evidence-rich and badly summarised.

There are at least four randomised controlled trials, three large field experiments, two systematic reviews, and an ongoing RCT programme at Karolinska. Real effect sizes exist: honeypot warnings cut entry from 75% to 35–47% (odds ratios 3.05–5.02); a dark-web intervention lifted help-click rates from 8.7% to 15.7%; Google's redesign cut follow-up searches by 3.8 percentage points. Nothing comparable exists anywhere in the humanitarian risk space.

Consequence: on this track you cannot win by being novel. You win by being more precise than the brief. A jury containing people who wrote these papers will reward a team that quotes the confidence intervals and knows where the evidence is weak.
2

The "1 in 100" in the brief is an average that hides a 700-fold range — and the range is the product.

Help-click rate across the four flagship deployments: Ahmia 12.4%, Google 0.73%, Intercept ~1%, Pornhub reThink 0.0175% (490 clicks from 2.8 million displays). Sorted, they line up with one variable: how many other things the user could click instead. Ahmia returns no results at all — help is the only exit. Google puts a box above a working results page. Pornhub leaves the entire site running with a close button.

Consequence: this reframes the whole challenge. The creative problem is not what the message says. It is what else is on the screen. Message copy moves the number by single digits; choice architecture moves it by two orders of magnitude. That is a defensible, novel, testable thesis and almost certainly no other team will bring it.
3

The single biggest trap on this track is in the organisers' own demo tooling.

tools/generate-traffic.js ships with baked outcome rates of 15% and 18% click-through against a 1% control. No deployment in the literature has produced 15% on a general-purpose platform. Ahmia managed 12.4% on a Tor search engine whose entire design is that help is the only available link. If a team demos a dashboard showing a 15× lift on a hobby forum, and a juror has read the Ahmia paper, that is the moment the pitch dies.

Consequence: build the demo against honest numbers — 0.5–1.5% baseline, 2–3× lift from choice architecture — and say out loud why. Nobody else will, and it converts the weakest part of the demo into the strongest part of the pitch.

My recommendation on one-or-both is in §12, and it turns on something neither brief says out loud: the two tracks reward opposite skills. ACAPS Concept scoring is 40% concept; UNICRI gives 25% to adoptability and 25% to safety. One is a design problem, the other is a shipping problem.

The thesis this gives you

Everyone in this field optimises the sentence. The 700× spread between deployments is not a sentence problem — it is a layout problem. A plug-and-play module can control layout in a way it can never control a platform's copy.

What this means concretely

The brief's own guiding question is "which format interrupts the moment best: a full-page warning, a pop-up, a chatbot, or something else?" The honest answer from the data is: the format that removes the alternatives. That is a defensible creative position, and it can be demonstrated in 20 seconds in a live demo by showing the same message in three choice architectures.

There is a real cost, and it has to be stated. Full replacement of a working site is hostile to the user and to the platform's own economics — which is exactly why Pornhub, Google and every commercial platform will never do it. So the practical design question becomes:

  • How much of the page can a small platform afford to give up? A hobby forum has almost no commercial stake in a single CSAM-adjacent search. A 20,000-member forum can afford a full interstitial that Pornhub cannot. The target platform in the brief is precisely the entity that can afford maximal friction. That is the argument for why this product exists at all rather than being built by the big platforms.
  • What can be added without subtracting? Not removing the results, but inserting a required interaction before they are revealed — a gate rather than a wall.

Four things in the kit that need correcting

  1. The "82% did not search again" statistic is weaker than it reads. The reThink evaluation is explicit that it analysed sessions, not users, that "the sessions are not distinct, which means that double-counting individuals occurred (although the extent of this cannot be quantified)", and that there was no pre-intervention baseline. The 82% figure is a within-session observation, not a behavioural outcome. The brief presents it as a headline success. Quote it with the caveat or you will be caught.
  2. The Ahmia "8.7% → 15.7%" is confounded, and the paper says so. At campaign launch the interface was also redesigned — several stacked panels were consolidated into a single warning page. The neutral control message alone scored 12.17%, well above the 8.73% pre-campaign baseline. So most of the lift is layout, not content. The paper states this plainly: "These changes may account for some portion of the increase in CTR." The genuine content finding is the within-campaign randomised comparison: harm-focused framing beat the other messages. That is the defensible claim.
  3. The brief's "harm-focused message worked best" is not settled across studies. In Prichard's 2022 honeypot trial, a harm message was the one condition that failed to reach significance (OR 1.31, 95% CI 0.72–2.39) while IP-trace and illegality warnings both worked. In the 2024 follow-up, therapeutic referral messages performed about as well as deterrence. In Ahmia, harm framing won on clicks to help. In self-report, behaviour-control and distress messages are rated best for help-seeking while legality and harm are rated best for deterrence. The honest synthesis is: framing effects are real but context-dependent, and the field does not yet know which frame wins where. "Nobody yet knows which message, format and timing works best for which group" is in the brief — take it seriously rather than assuming the answer.
  4. One citation in EVIDENCE.md is mislabelled. It records the third condition as "T1 (OR = 4.06) and T2 (OR = 3.05)" but the published paper names them T1 and T3. Harmless, but don't propagate it into a slide.
The gap in their message blocks

Every block is a statement of fact — it is illegal, it harms children, help exists, you are not alone, this is confidential. Not one block is a direct answer to the thing the user is telling themselves — "I'm only looking", "it's not a real child", "it doesn't count if I don't download it", "nobody's hurt by a picture". The entire cognitive-distortion literature (§06) says that is the operative mechanism, and the kit does not touch it. That is the largest unexploited space in the brief.

The papers, one by one

Nineteen sources, ordered from foundational to applied. For each: what it actually found, how strong it is, and what it changes.

A · The foundational epidemiology

Nurmi et al. (2024) — the Tor study that founded the field

Strong
Scientific Reports 14:7849 · Tampere University · one of the most-downloaded papers in the journal that year (340,967 downloads)
Design
Analysis of 176,683 onion domains (2018–2023), 238,794,231 search queries on Ahmia.fi, and 11,470 anonymous survey responses recruited at the point of the CSAM search.
Found
11.1% of search sessions (12.3M) explicitly sought CSAM. One in five onion sites shared CSAM in 2023; it was easily available on 21 of the 26 most popular Tor search engines, four of which advertised it on the landing page. Of searches that named an age: 40.5% targeted 11-year-olds or younger, 11% age 12, 8.2% age 13, 11.6% age 14, 10.9% age 15, 12.7% age 16. 65.3% first saw CSAM when they were under 18 themselves. Half first encountered it accidentally. 48.1% want to stop. Help-seeking correlated with duration and frequency of viewing, depression, anxiety, self-harm thoughts, guilt and shame — and 73.9% of those who sought help could not get it.
Strength
Enormous n, real-world behaviour, peer reviewed. Weakened by self-selection into the survey (respondents opted in mid-search) and by being a Tor-only population.
Changes
This is your problem-size slide and your moral argument in one source. "Three out of four people who went looking for help did not get it" is the strongest single sentence available on this track, and it is about routing, which is what the product actually is.

Insoll et al. (2022) — who goes on to contact children

Strong
Journal of Online Trust & Safety 1(2) · n = 1,546
Found
42% self-reported having sought direct contact with children online after viewing CSAM; 10% said weekly or nearly every time. 58% feared their viewing might lead to sexual acts. 70% had never told anyone. Frequency of use was the best predictor of contact-seeking. Also predictive: viewing material depicting infants and toddlers, contact with other CSAM users, older age of first exposure, and thoughts of self-expressing beforehand. Long duration alone, once frequency is controlled, was not predictive.
Strength
Large anonymous sample on a population that is otherwise invisible. Self-report on a criminal behaviour, so almost certainly an undercount — the authors say so.
Changes
It justifies the "In deep" persona and gives you a triage signal: frequency is the single best discriminator. A module that can distinguish a first search from a fifth search in a week is doing something behaviourally meaningful, not just cosmetically different.

Insoll et al. (2024) — why people don't get help the most actionable paper

Strong
Journal of Online Trust & Safety 2(4) · 18 Sept 2024 · n = 4,493 (main) + 266 active help-seekers
Found
55.0% want to stop. Of the 266 who actively tried to get help: 70.2% struggled to find an anonymous resource, 54.8% could not find information on relevant help, 43.9% reported difficulty finding a suitable service, and 40.4% were unable to access help at all. 27.2% of respondents are afraid to seek help. 72.2% feel at least sometimes unable to control their behaviour. Help-seeking rises with frequency of use — but falls for daily users (17.1%) versus weekly (18.0%), which is the counter-intuitive finding that matters for the "In deep" persona.
Strength
The help-seeker sub-sample is small and self-selected. The barriers, though, are large and consistent with Nurmi 2024's 73.9%.
Changes
This is the product thesis, stated numerically. The problem is not that people don't want help — 55% do. It is that seven in ten cannot find an anonymous resource when they look. A routing layer is not a nice-to-have; it is the missing 70%.

Lahtinen et al. (2025) — charged versus never-caught

Good
Child Abuse & Neglect 161:107299 · n = 2,384
Found
Compared 474 who self-reported charges for offences against children, 620 against adults, 1,290 with no charges. Prior violent-offence charges, online grooming, physical contact with children, and searching for material depicting infants/toddlers all predicted belonging to a charged group. Searching for material depicting boys specifically raised the odds of being in the child-offence group.
Strength
Solid, but the charged/uncharged split rests entirely on self-report and the sample is dark-web-recruited.
Changes
Two things. First, there exists a larger sub-population with no antisocial history, whom the authors explicitly describe as "more receptive to preventive measures" — that is your target. Second, it is a warning against designing for the worst case: the hardened minority will not click help, and designing the module around them wastes the intervention on the people it cannot reach.

The hardest fact in the entire literature

Ahmia ran a 140-day randomised field experiment on 3.1 million CSAM-related searches — and found no detectable reduction in CSAM searching whatsoever. Google found a 3.8 percentage-point reduction within a session and could not measure anything beyond it. Pornhub found a decline but had no baseline to compare against. Nobody has demonstrated that a warning reduces a person's CSAM use beyond the next few minutes.

Every pitch on this track will claim deterrence. The team that states this limitation first, and then designs specifically against it, is doing something the field has not done.

C · Treatment, and why routing beats persuasion

Lätth et al. (2022) — Prevent It, the first dark-web psychotherapy RCT

Strong
Internet Interventions · Karolinska Institutet · n = 160, randomised 1:1 against psychological placebo
Design
8-week therapist-supported internet CBT, anonymous, recruited globally from darknet forums. Placebo-controlled, single-blind, adequate results on trial-quality indices.
Found
Significant reduction in weekly CSAM viewing time: estimate −0.25 h, 95% CI −0.46 to −0.04, p = .017, d = 0.18. Fewer negative side effects than placebo. No severe adverse events. About half of participants with post-treatment ratings reported no CSAM use in the preceding week (but no between-group difference on that measure).
The problem
Attrition was severe. 29% of the treatment arm and 5% of placebo completed zero modules. Only 33 participants completed all eight (12 treatment, 21 placebo).
Changes
A small effect size (d=0.18) is still the difference between "there is no treatment for this population" and "there is one, and it works, and it is safe." But the dropout tells you that delivering someone into an 8-week programme is a much harder handoff than delivering them to a phone number. Design the route-to-help as a ladder, not a leap.

Prevent It 2.0 (2026) — the replication, and it is stronger

Strong
International waitlist-controlled RCT · NCT05831657, multicentre SE/DE/PT, funded by the European Commission · n = 203
Found
9-week iCBT. Reduction in sexual urges toward children of −6.41 scale points, 95% CI −8.26 to −4.56, d = −1.01; and −1.87 hours weekly spent viewing CSAM. Clinically significant reductions in an anonymous online sample. Participants were 92.1% male, 66.6% aged 21–40, roughly half Europe and 39% North America.
Changes
This is a much larger effect than the first trial and it is now replicated across three languages. The claim "treatment works" is no longer speculative — it is the strongest causal evidence anywhere in this challenge. Which means the weakest link in the whole system is no longer treatment. It is the 70% who cannot find the door.

Haubrock et al. (2026) — what police think of it

Useful

D · The behaviour-shape papers — habituation, and what to do about it

Two 2026 papers in Electronics by Watters, Scanlan and colleagues are the only formal treatment of the question the brief asks directly: "how do you avoid habituation when someone sees it for the fifth time?" Neither is in the organisers' reading list. Both should be.

PaperWhat it saysWhat to do with it
Watters, Scanlan & Jain (2026)
Electronics 15(17):4045 — dual-process sensitisation–habituation model		 Repeated exposure is governed by two opposing processes: sensitisation (perceived risk accumulates over a ~50-day memory) and habituation (attention depletes ~30% per exposure, recovers over ~10 days). Because they move in opposite directions with exposure spacing, per-warning effectiveness is non-monotone with an interior optimum — around 18 days under baseline calibration. Frequent warning buys risk at the cost of salience; sparse warning preserves salience but accumulates no risk. They then model polymorphic warnings (rotating variants) and show variety preserves salience — but only to the extent habituation is variant-specific rather than general. This is a specification, not a metaphor. Build a rotation engine with a minimum re-warning interval, variant-level salience tracking and a rotation policy. It is the single most technically distinctive thing you could demo, it answers a jury question the brief itself poses, and it is grounded in a peer-reviewed model rather than a hunch. Caveat: τ*≈18 days is the authors' illustrative mathematical optimum, not an empirically validated scheduling rule — say so.
Watters et al. (2026)
Electronics 15(11):2281 — narrative review of CSAM desistance via AI, chatbots and automated warnings		 Consolidates the RCT, honeypot, chatbot and operational evidence. Key distinctions: messages attributed to credible sources (law enforcement) outperform anonymous warnings; strongest effects where deterrent or therapeutic framing is paired with salient presentation; weaker or non-significant effects for less direct harm-based messaging. Explicitly separates short-term behavioural interruption from verified long-term desistance. Gives you the strongest single design rule available: attribute the warning. A warning from "the moderators of this forum" is weaker than one jointly attributed to Offlimits and the Dutch police. The kit's starter widget has no attribution at all.
Hunn et al. (2023)
AIC Trends & Issues 669 — how to implement online warnings		 The practitioner guidance paper behind the Best Practice Guide. Trigger points beyond keywords: flagged search terms, known CSAM URLs, hash matches on upload, grooming-detection activation. Directly expands the contract's type: search | chat | navigation into a richer trigger taxonomy. Low cost, obvious credibility gain.
Price et al. (2024)
Journal of Child Sexual Abuse 33(7):869–890 — review of digital interventions as secondary prevention		 The most sober review. Explicitly concludes: "Given the limited number of sources and variances in type and context, it is not possible to draw any broader conclusions as to the impacts of these digital interventions at this time." Your honesty beat. If a juror asks "does any of this actually work", the correct answer is "the most recent systematic review says we cannot yet conclude — which is precisely why the brief asks for a design that can be tested." That answer is better than an overclaim, and it is available to almost nobody else in the room.

The findings the evaluation is honest about

  • 82% of sessions did not search for CSAM again — but this is a within-session measure on non-distinct sessions, with double-counting acknowledged and unquantified, and no pre-intervention baseline existed.
  • The referral rate declined over time. The report attributes this to possible saturation — "the chatbot was less effective over time at garnering the interest of users." The helpline calls, however, increased over the period.
  • 99.83–99.87% of sessions never triggered the warning at all. So the intervention touched a tiny, non-random slice of the platform.
  • The chatbot handled text badly. Only 132 of ~1,650 conversations were exportable for analysis; 80% of users interacted via buttons, not text. The report's own conclusion is that future versions should "be more complex, enabling them to be empathetic without being therapeutic" and should connect directly to a live human chat.
  • Displacement was not ruled out. The report notes a user who stopped searching on Pornhub may simply have gone elsewhere: "possibly not desisting, but moving themselves to another outlet."
The strategic lesson, and it is uncomfortable

Pornhub has a warning on it because a payment network made its business model conditional on it, not because anyone proved the warning worked. That is the actual causal pathway for adoption at scale in this field — and it is not available to a 20,000-member Dutch hobby forum, which has no card network to lose.

So the plug-and-play product cannot borrow the big-platform adoption mechanism. It needs a different one for small platforms — which is precisely what the brief is asking you to invent. Naming this out loud in the pitch shows you understand how anything in this sector ever gets installed.

The four things reThink got right, which are worth stealing

  1. It took the search away and then offered a conversation. The chatbot was a bridge, not a leaflet. That is still the best structure anyone has deployed.
  2. It sat at the exact trigger point — the moment a banned search is entered, not a general awareness banner.
  3. It escalated from buttons to text and let the user choose the depth of engagement. 80% chose buttons, which is a design instruction, not a failure.
  4. It handed to a human. Every credible intervention in this literature terminates in a person, not a webpage.

The four-message structure — steal this exactly

Derived from research with people who have offended, and it is the cleanest thing in the entire field:

  1. Viewing sexual images of children, and having online sexual conversations with children, is a crime.
  2. It causes harm to children.
  3. There are consequences for the offender.
  4. But there is help available to stop and change.

Note what the structure does: it establishes wrongness, establishes harm, establishes that the wrongness attaches to the person without closing the door, then opens the door. The order matters. Put help first and it reads as a sales pitch; put consequences last and it reads as a threat with no exit.

The published criticism, and why it's actually an opening

Professor Sonia Livingstone (LSE) called the ~1% click-through "disappointing, given the scale of the problem," while noting the counterpoint that four in five of those who do seek support engage with it.

The six bodies of theory, and what each gives you

Neutralisation techniques — a copywriting spec

Unused in this field
Sykes & Matza (1957); applied to sexual offending by various authors
Theory
People who commit acts they know are wrong don't deny the act — they neutralise the guilt with a small set of standard justifications.
The five
Denial of injury ("nobody was hurt"); denial of victim ("they're not real / they consented / it's an image"); denial of responsibility ("I couldn't help it / it's an addiction"); condemnation of the condemners ("the police are the real criminals"); appeal to higher loyalties (rare here).
Application
Write one message block per neutralisation, each directly answering it. Not "this is illegal" but "a photograph is a record of a crime that was committed against a specific child, and every view is a market signal for another one." That is a denial-of-injury and denial-of-victim answer in a single sentence.
Why it wins
It is a testable content taxonomy where everyone else ships one generic paragraph. You can run eight variants against the eight distortions and claim you are testing a theory, not guessing.

Tainted fruit / contamination theory — the deterrence mechanism

Named in the papers, absent from the kit
Christenson (1992); Williams (2005); used as the theoretical frame in Prichard et al. 2024
Theory
A warning works not by threatening but by contaminating — it associates the target with something unpleasant, so the user's own desire is degraded. The object becomes less appealing rather than more dangerous.
Evidence
Prichard et al. explicitly ground their therapeutic-message effect in this: the message "taints" the user's perception and simultaneously "questions the excusability" of accessing it. The 2024 trials are the strongest test of it and both deterrent and therapeutic framings reliably contaminate.
Application
Explains why images didn't help in Prichard 2024 — a picture of an arrest adds threat but does not contaminate the object. What would contaminate: making the user picture the specific child, or the moment of the search. A message that makes the object feel sordid rather than wicked.
Why it wins
It gives you a principled reason to reject the obvious design (big red warning, handcuffs, "CRIMINAL OFFENCE"). That rejection is itself a creative point.

Situational crime prevention — the framework the field actually uses

Standard
Wortley & Smallbone (2012), Internet Child Pornography: Causes, Investigation and Prevention
Theory
Rather than changing the offender, change the situation: increase perceived effort, increase perceived risk, reduce anticipated rewards, reduce provocations, remove excuses.
Application
All five are available in a plug-in. Effort: a required click, a delay. Risk: attribution, IP-trace messaging (OR 2.64 in Prichard). Reward: contaminating the object. Provocation: n/a. Excuses: the neutralisation-answering copy.
Why it wins
It is the academic consensus frame for this exact problem and it maps one-to-one onto features you can build. Cite it and your feature list stops looking arbitrary.

Shame, stigma and why "help" is a scary word

Under-used
Insoll 2024; Jahnke 2018; Levenson & Grady 2019; Swaby & Lievesley 2023
Evidence
70% have never told anyone. 27.2% are afraid to seek help. Stigma is repeatedly identified as the primary recruiter barrier — including by police officers (Haubrock 2026). Help-seeking correlates with depression, anxiety, self-harm thoughts, guilt and shame, meaning the people most likely to click are the people in the most distress, which is both an opportunity and a duty of care.
Application
The word "help" implies you need help, which implies you are the kind of person who needs help. The literature suggests the route to help should be framed around control and self-direction, not rescue. Note that in self-report, behaviour-control and distress messages scored highest for help-seeking while harm and legality scored highest for deterrence — because "take back control" is a less stigmatising frame than "get help".
Why it wins
Most teams will use the word "help" four times in a button. The evidence says don't.

Habituation and dual-process — the timing problem

Unexploited
Watters, Scanlan & Jain 2026; plus the general security-warning literature ("from warning to wallpaper")
Evidence
Attention depletes ~30% per exposure and recovers over ~10 days; perceived risk accrues over ~50 days. The two move oppositely with spacing, producing an interior optimum (~18 days) where per-warning effectiveness peaks. Rotation of genuinely differentiated variants preserves salience — but cross-variant habituation erodes the benefit. And in the field, reThink's referral rate genuinely declined over 18 months.
Application
A rotation engine: variant pool, minimum re-warning interval, salience proxies (dwell time, acknowledgement latency), and a policy that escalates form rather than repeating content. Also: measure dwell time. The Watters paper recommends exactly this — "attentional salience should be measured independently rather than inferred solely from desistance."
Why it wins
It directly answers a question the brief itself asks, with a citation the jury probably hasn't read, and it produces a demoable mechanic rather than a slogan.

Motivational interviewing and the failure of confrontation

Translates cleanly
Miller & Rollnick; FRAMES model (Miller & Sanchez 1994); harm-reduction literature
Evidence
Across alcohol, drug and offending-behaviour interventions, confrontational approaches reliably increase resistance and reduce engagement; empathic, autonomy-supporting approaches work better even where the goal is abstinence. The Best Practice Guide's "avoid excessive punishment — overly punitive or shaming language can trigger defensiveness and reduce help-seeking behaviour" is this finding, restated.
Application
The FRAMES checklist is a literal message spec: Feedback (what you're doing and what it does), Responsibility ("the decision is yours" — matches Offlimits' own phrasing), Advice (one concrete next step), Menu (more than one route), Empathy (no judgement), Self-efficacy ("people do change"). Six boxes, each a sentence, all evidence-based, and no team in this hackathon will have heard of it.
Why it wins
It converts "make the copy nice" into a checklist you can score yourself against. And the self-efficacy element is already supported by your strongest causal evidence — Prevent It 2.0, d = −1.01.

The one distortion nothing in the world addresses

A live, growing, entirely unserved message gap

The IWF recorded 3,443 AI-generated CSAM videos in 2025 — a 26,385% rise — and 65% were the most severe category. Protect Children found 35% of surveyed CSAM users had viewed or created AI-CSAM, half of those commercially. Whether or not a synthetic image harms a depicted child, the belief attached to it is exactly the denial-of-victim neutralisation: "it isn't a real child, so nobody was hurt."

Every message in the starter kit, every message in the Ahmia campaign, and every message in Project Intercept assumes a photographed child. There is no published intervention anywhere that addresses the synthetic-material belief. Given that the Dutch legal definition turns on apparent age and explicitly covers digitally generated imagery, a Dutch-language AI-CSAM message block is both legally accurate and completely novel. That is the clearest unclaimed creative territory I found on either track.

Adjacent fields that translate

You asked for solutions to similar problems. Six fields have solved structurally identical problems — interrupting a compulsive behaviour at the moment of intent, without identifying the person. Each contributes something specific.

The synthesis across all six

Every field that has actually reduced a harmful behaviour at scale did it by changing the default and adding friction, with persuasion as a secondary layer. The CSAM prevention field has, so far, only done the persuasion layer. That is the strategic gap, and it is the same gap as the choice-architecture finding in §01 — approached from a different direction.

The Netherlands, precisely

The brief is Netherlands-first, and there are three things about the Dutch context that most teams will get wrong. Each is a small, cheap credibility gain.

1 · The law is narrower than the brief implies

Since 1 July 2024, CSAM falls under article 252 of the Wetboek van Strafrecht (replacing 240b), under the Wet seksuele misdrijven. Maximum 6 years; up to 9 years where the offence is committed habitually or professionally (art. 254 lid 1 sub c). The article covers distributing, offering, publicly displaying, producing, importing, exporting, acquiring, possessing — and crucially "zich de toegang verschaffen" (obtaining access), which is what criminalises viewing without downloading.

But two details in the prosecution guideline (2024R006) matter enormously for message copy:

  • "Zich toegang verschaffen" requires a deliberate act. Legal commentary is explicit: "Het zich toegang verschaffen veronderstelt dat dit welbewust gebeurt maar er is dus wel een actieve handeling vereist" — it presupposes the access is deliberate, and an active act is required. The guideline's base offence also contemplates "voorwaardelijk opzet" (conditional intent / dolus eventualis). This means copy that says "even accidentally viewing is a crime" is legally wrong. It also means the honest, defensible line is "knowingly searching for or viewing this material is a criminal offence" — which is both accurate and does the same persuasive work.
  • The sentencing reality is nothing like six years. The base offence for possession / acquisition / access to a small quantity, by a first offender, on a single occasion, with conditional intent, is: a short unconditional prison sentence + 240 hours of community service + a suspended sentence with special conditions. Where it is habitual or professional or repeated or within a knowingly CSAM environment: 12 months. There is a community-service prohibition (art. 22b Sr), so a task penalty can only be imposed alongside a custodial element. And since 1 July 2024 a probation period of up to ten years can be imposed.
Two ways to use this

For the message: "up to six years" is accurate but not credible to a user who has read about actual cases. A message that names the real consequence — a criminal record, up to 240 hours of community service, a suspended sentence with conditions, a probation period of up to ten years — is more deterrent, not less, because it is believable. Research on deterrence is consistent that certainty beats severity, and the certainty here is high.

For the pitch: knowing that maximum penalties are not typical outcomes is exactly the kind of detail that signals you read the prosecution guideline rather than the press release. And the ten-year probation period is the single most under-used fact on this track.

3 · The Dutch context has a specific threat model

  • Vigilantism is a live, recent phenomenon. In 2020, Dutch "pedojager" groups lured men into meetings and filmed or assaulted them; police counted around 250 violent incidents in a few months.   • This is a direct design constraint. Any tool that could be mistaken for identification or entrapment is dangerous. It is also a reason the module must be visibly, obviously not a vigilante tool — a clear statement of what it does not do, on the platform's own settings page.
  • It is also a powerful pitch line: "every warning we install is an alternative to a vigilante." The brief says it; almost no team will use it.

4 · The language problem the brief doesn't resolve

The module must work in Dutch and English. Offlimits operates in Dutch. So what happens when an English-speaking user on a Dutch forum triggers the warning? Two options, and you must pick one deliberately:

  • Route by country, translated — show Offlimits in English. Dishonest if the line cannot actually serve in English.

The first is correct and almost nobody will do it. A user who calls a Dutch helpline in English and gets turned away has been harmed by the intervention.

Why has nobody built it?

Four reasons, and each one is a design constraint:

  • Liability. Showing a warning implies the platform knew the behaviour was possible. A small operator may prefer silence. Answer: make the module's default state actively protective — installing it is evidence of care, and the docs should say so in the language a lawyer would accept.
  • No trusted message. A platform cannot invent legal copy; getting it wrong is worse than nothing. Answer: ship maintained, sourced, jurisdiction-specific copy so the platform never has to write it.
  • No proof it works. Covered at length above.
  • No one to hand off to. Routing requires knowing the local service. Answer: the routing table is the product, and it's the thing that scales by country.

The regulatory tailwind

The EU Child Sexual Abuse Regulation (CSAR, proposed May 2022) would require hosting and interpersonal-communication providers to carry out risk assessments and adopt proportionate mitigation measures, including measures relating to the design and operation of their services, reporting mechanisms and user safety — with different risk categories for different services and stronger obligations for higher-risk ones. The Council's November 2025 position retained mandatory risk assessment and mitigation and dropped mandatory detection orders.

Trilogue status: fifth trilogue ended without agreement in June 2026; a rejection motion in July fell short and the ePrivacy derogation was extended to 3 April 2028 by Regulation (EU) 2026/1881; a sixth trilogue was scheduled. It is not law.

So: do not build a compliance product for a regulation that does not exist. But do note, in one sentence, that risk-mitigation-by-design is the direction of travel — because it is the only mechanism by which the tail of platforms will ever be compelled to install anything, and it is the difference between a voluntary module and a mandated one.

Constraints that shape the answer

  • Team size 5, 26 hours on site, multiple submissions allowed.
  • ACAPS has no submission apparatus; UNICRI ships templates, a pitch guide and a published rubric. That asymmetry means the UNICRI track has a much lower coordination cost per unit of quality.
  • ACAPS needs a December pilot to be real. UNICRI needs an artifact that can be hosted on a UN platform.
  • Both tracks can be entered with the same 5 people. They cannot both be entered well.

My reading

The two tracks are not equally risky — they are risky in opposite directions.

ACAPS is a low-information-risk, high-execution-risk track. Almost nobody will do the quantitative work, so a team that does it wins on differentiation. But if you're wrong about expert participation, the whole premise collapses and you find out in December, not on Sunday.

UNICRI is a high-information-risk, low-execution-risk track. The evidence is unambiguous that warnings work in the short term and that routing is the binding constraint. But everyone in the room can build a modal, and a jury that includes people who wrote these papers will separate the teams that read them from the teams that didn't in about ninety seconds.

The recommendation

Do UNICRI as the primary submission, with ACAPS as a second pod only if you have five people and the ACAPS pod has its own lead.

Three reasons:

  1. The ceiling is higher and the floor is lower in the same direction. UNICRI's rubric is more forgiving of an imperfect demo (Clarity is only 10%) and more rewarding of craft (Adoptability 25 + Safety 20 = 45% on execution quality). ACAPS is 40% concept, which means a single strong idea can carry a weaker build — good, but it also means a strong build with a weak idea scores poorly, and the concept space is crowded by your own earlier work.
  2. The research asymmetry is in your favour here and against you there. On UNICRI, having read the 19 papers this week is a durable, defensible edge that no one can replicate in 26 hours. On ACAPS, the equivalent edge is your own dataset work, which is strong but is also the thing most likely to be independently rediscovered by another competent team running the starter notebook and going one step further.
  3. The artifact survives the weekend. An installable open-source module with honest numbers and an ethics-approved test protocol is something UNICRI can put on the AI for Safer Children hub. A BENCHMARK prototype needs ACAPS to say yes to a pilot before it does anything.

Counter-argument, stated fairly: if your five people include two strong quantitative analysts and one designer, the ACAPS pod is cheap to run and the December pilot is a real, rare prize. If you have to choose, choose UNICRI — but only if you actually build the thing and not a deck about the thing.
