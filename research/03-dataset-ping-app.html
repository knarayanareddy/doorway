Three answers

On the data

Not available — by deliberate choice
And the real number is 2,387× the one you were told
What exists
238,794,231 search queries and 110,133,715 search sessions from Ahmia.fi, February 2018 – February 2023 — plus a newer, larger analysis covering 2018–2024 at 171,262,465 queries / 132,778,744 sessions.
What's released
Only the survey: 11,470 responses, a 2.3 MB dataset and replication tutorial on Zenodo. The search logs are explicitly withheld.
The paper's own words
"Although the web search logs do not necessarily contain personal information, it seems feasible to combine or extract such information from the data; thus, we do not release the web search logs."
Verdict
Don't train on it. See §02 — it is unavailable, methodologically wrong for the task, forbidden by the brief, and unnecessary. Ask Nurmi for the statistics instead; they're worth more to you than the data.

On the expert ping

Good idea — build it
The best product idea to come out of the ACAPS track
Why it works
It converts judgement from a supply problem (analysts must go find experts) into a matching problem (the platform knows who knows what). Every forecasting system in the literature is pull-based, and pull-based systems leak participants.
The one correction
Self-declared expertise is an onboarding mechanism, not a weighting mechanism. Let people declare it — it gets them in the door — but weight by track record. People are reliably overconfident about what they know.
The mechanism to add
A fourth notification type nobody has built: "the statement you dissented on has been rewritten." That is the loop-closing that makes the whole thing retain, and it is the only feature that directly serves the "my dissent changed the paragraph" promise.

On the app

No native app. PWA for one track only.
Two hours, not two days
Doorway
No app at all. The intervention renders on the platform's website, which you don't control. An app of ours is irrelevant to the person being warned and is used once by the moderator. Spend the time on mobile-web rendering instead — most forum traffic is phones and almost every team will demo a desktop modal.
Benchmark
PWA, shipping with the web app. Ninety seconds is a phone-shaped task, and an installed PWA gets push notifications without an app-store review. Manifest plus service worker is about two hours; a native app is two days and two codebases and earns zero points on either rubric.
The rule
Never fork the codebase. One web app, plus a manifest.
Read this before anything else — you have a new paper to know about

While checking the data question I found a Nurmi paper that is not in the research dossier: Illicit Search Behavior and Responses to Filtering and Deterrence on Tor: Evidence from Ahmia (Nurmi, Paju, Arroyo, Scanlan & Patsakis), posted to SSRN on 5 March 2026. It is a 2018–2024 update and it contains three things that belong in the pitch:

  • CSAM search volume fell from 27.2% of Ahmia queries in 2018 to 6.0% in 2024 — the strongest real-world evidence that filtering, not messaging, moves the aggregate number.
  • 4.5% of search sessions redirected users to therapy resources.
  • A proposed "two-out-of-three" theorem for privacy-enhancing technologies: you can have only two of privacy, anonymous publishing, and absolute censorship resistance — and the authors argue for relaxing the third to create a moderation layer. That is a directly relevant design argument for a product that has to sit inside a privacy-preserving service.

Two cautions. It is an SSRN preprint, not peer reviewed. And its 4.5% is not comparable to the 12.44% in Ó Ciardha et al. — one is a share of all sessions, the other a share of warnings triggered. Don't put them in the same sentence without saying so.

Three datasets, one released

DatasetSizeReleased?How it was collected

(ii) Search engine web-server logs238.8M queriesNoAhmia's own logs. Withheld because re-identification is judged feasible. This is the dataset you were thinking of.

How the "sessions" were actually built — this matters for your idea

Ahmia stores no IP addresses and sets no cookies. So the paper reconstructs sessions heuristically: from HTTP referer metadata, assuming a new query within five minutes belongs to the same session. That is a defensible method for counting, and it is not a person-level identifier. It also means the "sessions" in the data are inferred chains, not observed users — which quietly undermines the premise of training a behaviour-classifier on them.

The paper is honest about this in its own data statement: the logs "do not necessarily contain personal information," but "it seems feasible to combine or extract such information from the data." That is the reason for withholding, and it is a good reason.

What is actually on Zenodo

Record 10.5281/zenodo.10457587 (version record 10457588), published 4 January 2024. Contents: protocol_exchange.zip (108.7 kB) containing protocol.md, twelve per-question CSVs, and survey_data.csv at 2.3 MB. Twelve single-answer questions on thoughts, feelings and actions related to CSAM use; designed to inform an anonymous CBT rehabilitation programme.

It is usable right now, this weekend. It is a cross-sectional survey of 11,470 respondents with coded single-choice answers — which means it supports exactly the kind of analysis a hackathon can actually finish: distributions, cross-tabs, and a help-seeking model. It does not support training a language model, and it was never going to.

The reframe worth having

Luigi — you were told about 100,000 interactions. The truth is bigger and more useful: there are 238 million queries you cannot have, and 11,470 survey responses you can have today. The second number is the one that fits inside a weekend, and it is the one the entire prevention field was built on. Every headline statistic in the brief — 55% want to stop, 42% sought contact, 70% never told anyone — comes from that survey or its siblings.

02

Why training an LLM on it is the wrong move — four grounds

This is a good instinct pointed at the wrong target. The instinct is right: there is a large behavioural corpus and nobody has mined it. The problem is that the corpus is unavailable, the task is ill-posed, the output is forbidden, and the alternative is stronger.

1 · Unavailable

The data does not exist outside Tampere, and deliberately. The authors withheld the logs on re-identification grounds. There is no licence, no repository, no API. The only route is a direct collaboration request to Nurmi — who happens to be a mentor at this event — and he will say no to "train a classifier to identify offenders," for reasons he has already published.

2 · Ill-posed

There is no behaviour in a search query, and the "sessions" are inferred. A query is one to five tokens. What you would want to model — escalation, persistence, intent — lives in sequences, and the sequences here are reconstructions from a referer header and a five-minute rule with no cookie and no IP. So a model trained on them would be learning to predict the heuristic, not the person. Add that CSAM-seeking is 11.1% of sessions: a classifier at that base rate, on reconstructed data, will mostly be detecting the reconstruction.

3 · Forbidden

It breaks the brief on three separate lines. "Should not: identify, track or expose individuals." "Must: protect the privacy of the people who see it." And safety rule 2: "No vigilante tooling: nothing that identifies, exposes or tracks individuals." A model that scores a person as likely to offend is the definition of the thing all three sentences prohibit. It is not a grey area and a jury containing UNICRI will not treat it as one.

4 · Unnecessary

The best predictor in the literature is a counter, not a classifier. Insoll et al. 2022, n=1,546: frequency of use is the strongest predictor of subsequent contact-seeking — better than duration, better than age of onset, better than any content feature. You do not need a neural network to count. And Lahtinen 2025 (n=2,384) found the majority of all groups — charged and uncharged — were never sanctioned, which means the population you would be trying to "detect" is most of the population. A detector that fires on everyone is not a detector.

And one practical trap

Even the queries are abuse-related text. The brief's first safety instruction is absolute: never search for, open, download or share CSAM, and never type abuse-related terms into real platforms. A training pipeline that ingests term lists and generates examples is exactly the kind of thing that gets a team disqualified rather than praised. There is no version of this that is safe to build in a 48-hour hackathon on a shared laptop.

03

What to do with the instinct instead

Four alternatives, in descending order of value. The first is worth more than the LLM would have been.

1 · Ask Nurmi for the statistics, not the data — highest value, costs one conversation

He has published parameter estimates that no one else has. Take them straight into the pitch:

NumberValueWhy it belongs in the deck
CSAM share of Ahmia queries, 2018 → 202427.2% → 6.0%The best available evidence that filtering, not messaging, moves the aggregate. It reframes the whole field — and it is a number almost nobody in the room will know.
Sessions redirected to therapy resources4.5%A real-world redirect rate measured over six years. Careful: not comparable to Ó Ciardha's 12.44%, which is a share of warnings triggered.
CSAM-seeking vs illicit-marketplace seeking5×Users are five times more likely to look for CSAM than for drugs. Kills any assumption that this is a niche behaviour.
Verified CSAM domains, Aug–Dec 202435,546Of 47,116 domains across 6,050 distinct services. Scale, from the most recent crawl.

Questions to actually ask him, in order: (1) is the 4.5% a click rate or an automatic redirect? (2) does the 2018→2024 decline survive controls for the filter expansions, or is it partly definitional? (3) would he endorse quoting the two-out-of-three theorem in a platform-facing product? Each answer is worth ten minutes of reading.

2 · Use the Zenodo dataset for a real, finishable analysis

11,470 responses, twelve coded questions, published with a replication tutorial. What you can honestly do in a few hours: reproduce the help-seeking distribution, cross-tabulate help-seeking against intensity of use, and produce one chart that shows the gap between wanting to stop and having sought help — with your own computation behind it rather than a citation.

Caveat, and say it out loud

It is a convenience sample of Tor users who both searched for CSAM and chose to answer a 20-minute survey. The authors say so explicitly. So use it for the shape of the problem, not for population inference. One sentence in the pitch — "this is a self-selected dark-web sample, so treat the magnitude as indicative and the direction as real" — buys you more credibility than the chart does.

3 · Put the ML somewhere it is welcome

There is a legitimate machine-learning contribution inside the brief, and it is not user classification. It is message-to-outcome at the aggregate level: given variant, format, trigger class and exposure bucket, which response produces a higher click-through? That is a small, honest model over counts — no personal data, no individual scores, and it produces the one thing the field has never had: a ranking of message × format × context. It also happens to be exactly the kind of thing a jury from UNICRI would call a real contribution rather than a demo.

4 · Keep the behaviour signal you already have

Doorway's Shift already tracks n = 5 in the browser. That is behavioural: it says this browser has seen the module five times, which is the strongest predictor in the literature, rendered as an integer, on the device, never transmitted. Frequency without identification. That is the technically honest version of what an LLM would have been reaching for.

"The single best predictor of escalation in this literature is how often someone does it. We measure that — as a number in your own browser that never leaves it. We don't need a model to know you've been here five times, and we shouldn't have one." Ten seconds of Q&A, if anyone asks why there's no ML
04

The ping: evaluating the expert-notification idea

The idea, restated as a product thesis

Experts declare the regions and topics they actually know. When a risk statement lands in one of those domains, they get told — instead of having to remember to go looking. Judgement becomes a matching problem rather than a supply problem. That is a genuinely better framing than anything I gave you on the ACAPS track, and it is the strongest argument for the panel existing that we have.

Why it works — four independent reasons

ReasonEvidence and mechanism
Every system in this literature is pull-based, and pull leaksMetaculus, Good Judgment, Cultivate, and ACAPS' own matrix all require the expert to remember to come back. The failure record is unambiguous: the X-risk forecasting tournament saw 51% expert dropout against 19% for superforecasters; the RAND forecasting programme was eventually shut down; and in ACAPS' own corpus 73% of the triggers carrying a completion rate carry no written criterion to complete. Every one of those is a system where the participant has to initiate.
Push collapses the marginal cost to near zeroThe reason a busy country analyst does not fill in a matrix is not that they cannot estimate a probability in ninety seconds. It is that they have to remember, find the link, and log in. A notification removes all three. That is the entire difference between a criterion nobody can act on and one they can.
Matching is the one thing a platform can do that a spreadsheet cannotThe risk statements already carry country and theme. So "a question in your field" is not a marketing promise — it is a query. That is more than Metaculus can do, because Metaculus has no idea what you know.
It solves the analyst's real problemThe analyst currently has to know who to ask as well as what to ask. Matching removes a whole class of judgement from the person least able to make it, and it produces a defensible answer to "why did you ask this person?"

Two failure modes it will hit, and the design fix for each

F1 · Self-declared expertise is overconfident

The problem. People reliably overestimate what they know, and the more confident they are the worse the calibration tends to be. If you weight by self-declared expertise you build a panel of the confidently wrong — which is worse than a random panel, because the errors correlate and the dispersion looks reassuringly tight.

The fix, which is the key insight in this whole section

Self-declared expertise is an onboarding mechanism, not a weighting mechanism. Let people tick their regions and themes — the declaration is what gets them in the door and what makes the notification feel personal, and that is its real job. Then ignore the declaration entirely when you weight, and weight by realised track record instead: how well has this person scored on the statements they were notified about? Display each expert's own calibration curve back to them. That is Good Judgment's actual mechanism — scores, not self-report — and it is the only thing in the literature that has been shown to improve a panel over time.

F2 · Notification fatigue kills it in week three

The problem. An expert who ticks five regions and three themes will, at a few hundred statements a month, receive a notification every couple of hours. They will not answer more — they will mute the app, and then uninstall it. Every push-based product dies this way, and public-sector tools die faster because the user has no reason to stay.

The fix

Four rules, and they should be visible on the settings screen as commitments:

  • A weekly cap, default three. Not "you'll get notified" — "you will get at most three a week, ever." A hard promise is what makes the notification safe to accept.
  • Batch into one digest by default, with an instant option for the highest-severity statements only.
  • "Not this week" is one tap and does not reduce your standing or your match score.
  • A notification must open a ninety-second task. Never a form. Notification-to-completion ratio is the health metric, and it should be on the analyst dashboard like any other.
The deepest risk, and it is a framing risk not a design one

This turns a forecasting product into a notification product. Notifications are saturated, unglamorous, and every juror has been annoyed by one. The pitch must never describe it as "we send push notifications." It is the dispatch layer — the thing that makes judgement arrive where the analyst needs it without asking anyone to log in. Name the feature the Ping if you build it; describe the capability as dispatch.

05

Designing the ping properly

The version you floated has one notification type. The product needs four, and the fourth is the one that makes it retain.

#TriggerWho receives itWhy it exists, and when to suppress it
1A new statement in your domain
"A new risk for the Sahel was published. Ninety seconds."		Everyone who declared that region or themeThe basic case from your message. Suppress if it is their fourth this week, or if the statement failed the gate — never notify anyone about a statement that hasn't passed quality control, because their first task will be a bad one and they will not come back for a second.
2You are the reliable one on something contested
"Panel is split on this one. Your last four calls on this country were well calibrated."		Only experts with a track record in that domainThis is where the panel earns its cost. A split panel is the highest-information state and the one most likely to be wrong; sending it to the best-calibrated people is the cheapest quality gain available. Never reveal the split before the estimate — that is anchoring.
3Your estimate is an outlier
"Your estimate sits outside the panel. Do you want to say why?"		The divergent expert only, after the factConverts a statistical anomaly into a rationale, which is the only thing that can explain a surprising number to an analyst. Must be after submission and private — never framed as "you're wrong".
4Your dissent was acted on
"You said this was two risks. It has been split. Here are both."		The dissenting expertThe retention mechanism. Nobody in this field closes the loop — an expert who objects into silence does not object twice, and then does not forecast twice. This is also the only feature that literally delivers "my dissent changed the paragraph," which is the strongest emotional promise in the ACAPS pitch. If you build one notification type, build this one.

How it changes the product

Three structural changes, all of them improvements:

  1. A new screen: the Declaration. Regions × themes, as a grid the expert fills in on first use, in about two minutes. It doubles as the reason the notification is welcome — a notification about your subject reads as a service; a generic one reads as spam.
  2. The card becomes phone-first. A ninety-second task is a phone-shaped task, and the card must render in one viewport. This is the real argument for a PWA, below.
  3. A new analyst metric: dispatch health. Notifications sent, opened, completed, and muted — per domain. If a domain's completion rate falls below a threshold, stop notifying into it and tell the analyst the domain is uncovered. A panel that quietly has nobody in a region is worse than a known gap, because ACAPS cannot see it.
The honest counter-argument, because a juror will make it

"This is a mailing list with extra steps." The answer has to be structural rather than defensive: a mailing list has no resolution, no scoring and no dissent. The ping is only a product because it opens onto a card that gets scored against an outcome weeks later and a dissent channel that can rewrite the statement. Take those away and it is indeed a mailing list — which is precisely why the ping alone is not the idea, and why it has to be attached to the gate and the dissent loop.

06

App, web, or PWA — decided per product

The tempting answer is "both, it shows ambition." That is the wrong answer on both rubrics, and the reasoning is different for each product.

The framework: what surface does each user actually touch?

ProductWho the user isSurface you controlWhat an app would actually be for
DoorwayTwo: the moderator who installs it, and the person who sees the warningThe person sees it on the platform's own website — a surface you do not control and cannot replaceNothing. An app of ours cannot host the intervention, because the intervention happens inside someone else's search box. The moderator uses the settings screen once, on a laptop.
BenchmarkExperts on their phones, briefly and repeatedlyThe card, which you fully controlGenuinely useful — ninety seconds, several times a week, arriving as a notification. This is the textbook PWA case.

Why not native, for either

Cost of a native appReality at hour 20 of a 26-hour event
Two codebasesYou have one team. A second client is roughly 60% of the first one's cost, for a product that does nothing the web version can't.
Store reviewApple review is not instant and a first submission can bounce. You cannot demo a build that is "in review".
Signing, provisioning, buildsA day of setup for a device that half the jury does not have.
AccountsAn app forces identity in a way the web does not — and on Benchmark, the promise is that peers never see your name. Adding an identity system to a product about anonymity needs a plan, not an afternoon.
ScoringZero points on either rubric. UNICRI's adoptability is about the platform switching on, not the expert's phone. ACAPS concept criteria do not reward a second client. And §06/§09 of the product review already found the two fatal risks are claim-narrowing, not feature count.

The two-hour PWA path (Benchmark only)

20 min · build

Manifest

manifest.json with name, icons, display: standalone, theme colour, and a start URL that goes straight to today's card. Add the link tag. Done.

45 min · build

Service worker

Cache the shell so the card opens instantly and works on a bad connection — which matters, because these are experts in field offices. This is the whole reason to do it.

30 min · build

Push, with a fallback

Web push via the service worker. State the constraint plainly: on iOS, push only works for PWAs that have been added to the home screen, and the permission prompt can only be triggered by a tap. So ship the email fallback on day one and treat push as an upgrade.

25 min · build

Install prompt at the right moment

Never on first load. Offer it after the expert's second completed card — the point at which they have proven they'll come back. An install prompt before value is a bounce.

And for Doorway, spend that time on mobile web instead

The gate will be seen on a phone far more often than on a desktop — forum traffic is majority mobile. Almost every team will demo a desktop modal. Three cheap things that will be visible in a demo:

  • A real phone in the pitch. Load the mock forum on a handset, trigger the gate, and show it fitting one screen. Ten seconds, and it says "we thought about the actual user" more clearly than any slide.
  • Thumb reach. The one action button must be in the lower third, above 44px, with the dismissal small and deliberate. Getting this wrong turns a gate into an obstacle.
  • One screen, no scroll, at 320 px wide. If the gate needs scrolling, the message is too long — which is a copy problem the gate will expose for free.
The decision, stated once

One codebase. Web for both. Manifest plus service worker for Benchmark only — two hours, not two days. No native app, and say why if asked: the intervention lives on someone else's website, and ninety seconds lives on a phone but not in an app store.

The one sentence that ties all three answers together

All three questions are the same question: where does the intelligence go? Not into a model that profiles a person, not into an app that duplicates a website, and not into a notification that fires for its own sake. It goes into the routing — which statement reaches which expert, which message reaches which person, and which decision was made because somebody objected. Every answer above is a decision to keep the intelligence in the plumbing rather than in a score.
